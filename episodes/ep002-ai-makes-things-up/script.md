# EP002 — Why Does AI Make Things Up?

Status: **draft for owner approval.** About 160 words, roughly 60 s. About 950 characters, which is roughly 950 ElevenLabs credits.

## Hooks
- **A (recommended, used below):** "Ask an AI for a source, and it might give you one that doesn't exist. Title, author, even page numbers."
- **B:** "AI can invent a study that never happened, and describe it with total confidence."
- **C:** "Why does AI sometimes make things up, and sound completely sure about it?"

## Narration (one line = one audio file / one paragraph)
| # | Beat | Line |
|---|---|---|
| 01 | Hook | Ask an AI for a source, and it might give you one that doesn't exist. Title, author, even page numbers. |
| 02 | Hook | It isn't lying. It's doing what it was built to do. |
| 03 | Curiosity | Here's why AI sometimes makes things up. |
| 04 | Explain | A language model writes by predicting the next token, using patterns learned from huge amounts of text. |
| 05 | Explain | Facts that appeared again and again leave strong patterns, so those answers are usually right. |
| 06 | Explain | But rare facts, like a small study or an obscure date, leave weak patterns. The model still has to write something, so it writes what a right answer would sound like. |
| 07 | Name it | Researchers call this a hallucination. |
| 08 | Why it persists | And many tests used to train and rank AI reward a confident guess over "I'm not sure." |
| 09 | Nuance | Tools that search the web or your documents first can help. But no AI is right every time. |
| 10 | Takeaway | So use AI for ideas and drafts. For facts that matter, check the source, and make sure it's real. |

## Accuracy notes
- Line 02, "isn't lying": lying implies intent. The model has none; it generates likely text.
- Lines 05–06 simplify the finding that accuracy tracks how often a fact appears in training data (Kandpal et al. 2023; Kalai et al. 2025).
- Line 08 is supported by Kalai et al. 2025: binary-graded benchmarks reward guessing over abstaining.
- Line 09 says retrieval reduces hallucination but doesn't eliminate it (Shuster et al. 2021; Kandpal et al. 2023).
